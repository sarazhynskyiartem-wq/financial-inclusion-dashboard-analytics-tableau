# Financial Inclusion in Low and Middle Income Countries

Illustration of the most recent data on financial inclusion around the world.

## About
The goal of this project is to demonstrate the availability of banking and financial 
technologies across countries, population access to basic financial instruments, 
and how these processes have evolved over time. All countries were divided into two 
groups for analysis: high-income countries and all other regions, considering only 
countries below the high-income threshold.

The data provides a broad picture of the current situation, highlights key insights, 
and offers recommendations for improving financial inclusion in line with the UN 
Sustainable Development Goals.

## Analytics & Conclusions
Traditional banking infrastructure has proven unnecessary for financial inclusion — 
at least in parts of the world. Mobile payment systems, rather than conventional 
banking, have become the dominant financial tool across most of Africa. Importantly, 
mobile money transactions can be performed using a basic feature phone — this 
technological leap allowed the region to skip an entire stage of traditional 
banking infrastructure development.

The use of classic banking instruments such as debit and credit cards has also grown 
significantly over the studied period, particularly in countries that moved from a 
lower to a higher income level.

The digital payments chart reveals another interesting insight: the average adoption 
rate in high-income countries (29.29%) and East Asia & Pacific (28.04%) is 
approximately the same. This can be explained by the underdeveloped infrastructure 
in remote parts of the Pacific region, where digital payments are practically the 
only available option for financial transactions. Sub-Saharan Africa lags 
significantly behind on this metric, which directly reflects the region's overall 
level of economic development, financial infrastructure coverage, and financial 
literacy.

The data shows that financial inclusion is developing unevenly: in regions where 
banking infrastructure never fully formed, mobile money has filled the gap more 
effectively than traditional institutions. This suggests that for low-coverage 
regions, the priority should not be building a banking network, but reducing 
barriers — both regulatory and technological — for mobile payment systems.
